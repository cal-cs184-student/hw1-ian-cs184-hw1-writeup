00 / OVERVIEW

Making images one sample at a time.

This homework builds a simplified SVG rasterizer. I began with single-color triangles, then added subpixel coverage, hierarchical transforms, smoothly varying vertex colors, texture lookup, and mipmap selection.

The throughline is sampling. Geometry decides where a pixel is covered; barycentric coordinates tell me what to interpolate there; texture filtering decides which detail the pixel can display. Working through each stage made the different sources of aliasing much clearer to me.

01 / SINGLE-COLOR TRIANGLES

Where is inside?

Triangle coverage starts with three directed edges and a sample at each pixel center.

The approach

I compute the smallest axis-aligned bounding box around the triangle, clip it to the framebuffer, and visit only pixel centers within it. For each center at (x + 0.5, y + 0.5), three edge functions test which side of each directed edge it lies on. I normalize the signs to handle clockwise and counterclockwise vertex order, and use inclusive edge comparisons before filling the pixel.

EDGE TESTE(A, B, P) = (Bₓ − Aₓ)(Pᵧ − Aᵧ) − (Bᵧ − Aᵧ)(Pₓ − Aₓ)

The work is at most O(W × H) for a bounding box W pixels wide and H pixels tall. A small triangle never triggers a scan of the whole framebuffer. Handling both winding orders and samples on edges were the main coverage cases to get right.

Extra credit: faster edge evaluation

I rewrote each edge function as Ax + By + C, calculated its coefficients once, and incremented values along each scanline. A triangle intersects a scanline in one interval, so the scan can stop after leaving a covered run. I measured an improvement with these optimizations; I will add the labeled timing comparison when I update the results.

02 / SUPERSAMPLING

More samples. Smoother edges.

Subpixel coverage turns a binary edge decision into an estimate of how much of a pixel is covered.

Buffer and resolve

At sample rate N, each pixel owns a regular √N × √N grid of sample colors. I store those N colors contiguously in a width × height × N sample buffer. Triangle rasterization tests each subpixel center and writes only covered samples. Points and lines fill all samples of an output pixel, since those primitives are not supersampled here. After drawing, resolve_to_framebuffer() averages a pixel's samples into one RGB value.

SUBPIXEL CENTERxₛ = x + (sₓ + 0.5)/√N   ·   yₛ = y + (sᵧ + 0.5)/√N

The buffer must resize when the viewport or sample rate changes. A single center sample can miss a skinny corner entirely. Four samples permit quarter steps in coverage; sixteen permit sixteenth steps. Averaging covered and uncovered samples softens edges, at roughly proportional cost in storage and coverage tests.

03 / TRANSFORMS

A pose built from joints.

A running Cubeman makes transform order and nesting visible.

For my_robot.svg, I designed a runner moving to the right: a forward-leaning torso and head, one raised knee, one extended leg, and arms swinging opposite the legs. Different body colors distinguish the moving components.

Each limb begins with a shoulder or hip translation, then a rotation of the upper limb. Translating to the elbow or knee creates a child frame, where a second rotation bends the lower limb. This hierarchy lets a shoulder carry its forearm along without manually changing every polygon coordinate. The actual SVG and renderer capture are still awaiting the supplied assets.

04 / BARYCENTRIC COORDINATES

Three vertices. Every color between.

Barycentric coordinates turn vertex attributes into values at any point inside a triangle.

A point inside a triangle is a weighted combination of its vertices. The weights α, β, γ sum to one. At a vertex, its own weight is one; across the triangle, the weights change smoothly. I use the same weights to interpolate RGB vertex colors and, later, UV texture coordinates.

INTERPOLATIONP = αV₀ + βV₁ + γV₂   ·   C(P) = αC₀ + βC₁ + γC₂

The custom red/green/blue triangle should show a smooth three-way blend. The walkthrough describes basic/test7.svg as a color wheel of adjoining interpolated triangles; compatible shared vertex colors should make its gradient appear continuous. The captures below are still pending.

05 / PIXEL SAMPLING

Finding color between texels.

Texture filtering changes the appearance inside triangles; supersampling changes their edges.

I interpolate a textured triangle's (u, v) coordinates with barycentric weights, then use that location to fetch a texture color. Nearest sampling selects the closest texel. Bilinear sampling blends the four neighboring texels using the sample's fractional position between them.

01 TEXELNearest

One lookup; fast, with abrupt transitions when enlarged.

04 TEXELSBilinear

Four lookups and interpolation; smoother between texels.

Magnified, high-contrast texture details should reveal the largest difference. More samples per pixel soften triangle boundaries, but nearest lookup can still look blocky inside. Bilinear lookup smooths the interior even at one sample per pixel. In nearly uniform areas, the two methods may appear similar.

06 / LEVEL SAMPLING

Choosing the right detail.

Mipmaps address too many texture details competing for too few screen pixels.

I compute UV coordinates at a sample and one pixel away in x and y. After scaling those differences into level-zero texel units, the larger texture footprint gives ρ; log₂(ρ) estimates the needed mipmap level. L_ZERO always uses the original texture. L_NEAREST selects the closest prefiltered level. My walkthrough also describes L_LINEAR, which blends two levels and gives trilinear filtering when paired with bilinear pixel sampling.

LEVEL OF DETAILL = log₂(max(‖ΔUVₓ‖, ‖ΔUVᵧ‖))   in level-zero texels

I chose UC-Berkeley-Symbol.png for the planned comparison: its seal has large shapes alongside fine lettering, dots, and thin lines. When minified, those details should make level selection visible. The source PNG, adapted SVG, and captures have not been supplied, so this remains a hypothesis to check.
